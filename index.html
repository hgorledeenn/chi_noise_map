Chicago's "Train Tax", Calculated

The Windy City is one of the few major cities with urban rapid train networks that run primarily above ground. For Chicago residents, the network provides much needed connection. But get too close and you won't hear the end of it (literally).

By Holden Green  ·  August 2026

Dylan Moring has always wanted to live in a high-rise building. After college, the 33-year-old spent time in both Boston and Denver, but it wasn't until he moved to Chicago that his dream could come true. Thanks, in no small part, to the Chicago Transit Authority train system.

Chicago’s urban rail network – known to Chicagoans as the “L” – is made up of eight distinct lines serves nearly half a million riders every week day. The L follows a “hub-and-spoke” model, meaning that most of its lines start outside the downtown and bring riders to the central business district. When the train lines meet in the city’s center, they follow a circular path around the downtown, which many residents call the “Loop”.

Unlike urban rail networks in most other major cities, the Chicago L runs mostly above ground. Only about 12% of the system’s 146 stations are underground. In New York City and Washington D.C., by comparison, more than half the stations are underground.

There are downsides to the underground systems embraced in New York and DC. Summertime often brings complaints of oppressive heat, as train AC systems pump hot air out of their cars and into the tunnel. Year round, flooding has become an ever-increasing issue as climate change contributes to more frequent and intense storms.

One positive effect of putting subway stations underground, though, is that it significantly reduces the sound of the train for people on the surface.

As Chicago’s L travels through the city, it winds its way over city streets and alleyways, above parks and the Chicago River. And, all along its journey, it runs as close a few feet from the second and third floors of residential buildings.

There’s a shared understanding among Chicago residents that the path of the CTA’s trains creates a “train tax” effect: put simply, you can save some money on rent or a mortgage every month if you can put up with the noise from the closely-passing trains.

For Moring, whose move to Chicago from Denver was motivated largely by a desire to be in a bigger-feeling city, the train tax worked to his benefit.

The sound from the train can reach about 85 decibels in Moring’s apartment when the windows are open. That’s about equivalent to a food blender or a leaf blower being used nearby.

When the windows are closed, that sound can drop as low as 60dB – closer to a normal conversational speaking volume, but still noticeable.

Now that he is used to it, though, Moring doesn’t mind the sound as much.

“It definitely took a little bit of adjustment, especially at night,” Moring said. “But I think we got used to it pretty quick.”

He thinks his adaptability to the noise has translated into direct savings on an otherwise great apartment.

“It felt kind of like a market discrepancy that we could take advantage of,” Moring said. “A lot of other buildings built in the same period of time that have three bedroom units are priced at least $1,000 a month more.”

While Moring isn’t alone in his noise tolerance; there are others who set a slightly further minimum distance for how close to the train they’re willing to live.

When Natalie Trumbo moved to Chicago in 2015, she was excited to take advantage of the accessibility offered by living near the L. A transplant from upstate New York, Trumbo’s first apartment maximized this: she was in a ground floor unit that directly backed onto the Red line’s Bryn Mawr station.

“It was super convenient to walk out of the alley and get on the train,” Trumbo said. “So that was a positive. But I only did it for a year.”

While she reached some level of comfort with the noise for herself, she decided she would sacrifice some of the accessibility in order to reduce the noise from the train she was experiencing in her next home. Plus, Trumbo, 43, and her husband had other priorities.

They now have three kids all under seven-years-old, and Trumbo commutes to the northern suburbs for her work, not into the Loop. It’s far more convenient, she explained, to just take their minivan around the city when they need to, and to not have to worry about bringing her young kids and their strollers onto the train.

Despite this, she said it’s still important to her that her children grow up experiencing the city and feeling comfortable on public transportation.

“I don’t want them to be afraid of it,” Trumbo said, “and I want them to be familiar with it and feel comfortable with it.”

After Trumbo and her husband had their third child, they decided to upsize from their current 2-bedroom condo on the 1500 block of W. Walton. Still on their list of priorities in the home search was transit accessibility, but Trumbo was no longer looking for a unit as close as her first Chicago apartment.
