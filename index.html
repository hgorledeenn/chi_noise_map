Chicago's "Train Tax", Calculated

The Windy City is one of the few major cities with urban rapid train networks that run primarily above ground. For Chicago residents, the network provides much needed connection. But get too close and you won't hear the end of it (literally).

By Holden Green  ·  August 2026

Dylan Moring has always wanted to live in a high-rise building. After college, the 33-year-old spent time living in both Boston and Denver, but it wasn't until he moved to Chicago that his dream could come true. Thanks, in no small part, to the Chicago Transit Authority train system.

The CTA system, unlike urban rail networks in most other major cities, runs mostly above ground. Only about 12% of the system’s 146 stations are underground. In New York City, by comparison, roughly 60% of stations are underground. More than half of Washington D.C.’s Metro stations are underground.

There are downsides to the underground systems embraced in New York and DC. Summertime often brings complaints of oppressive heat, as train AC systems pump hot air out of their cars and into the tunnel. Year round, flooding has become an ever-increasing issue as climate change contributes to more frequent and intense storms.

One positive effect of putting subway stations underground, though, is that it significantly reduces the sound of the train for people on the surface.

As the CTA’s train network – or, as it’s better known to Chicagoans, the “L” – travels through the city, it winds its way over city streets and alleyways, above parks and the Chicago River. And, perhaps most notably, it runs within feet in many cases of the second and third floors of apartment and condo buildings.

There’s a shared understanding among Chicago residents that the path of the CTA’s trains creates a “train tax” effect: put simply, you can save some money on rent or a mortgage every month if you can put up with the noise from the closely-passing trains.

For Moring, whose move to Chicago from Denver was motivated largely by a desire to be in a bigger-feeling city, the train tax worked to his benefit.
